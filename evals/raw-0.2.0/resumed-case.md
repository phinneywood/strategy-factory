## 2. Evidence and alternatives

### Evidence update — 2026-09-21
Source: user-supplied pilot report in this conversation. The quarterly figure is a projection from the pilot, not an observed full quarter of release. No queue-time result, actual cost, measurement detail, or staff-preference evidence was supplied.

The unchanged error rate is an observed result, not proof that the assistant caused no harm or preserved quality. Without a comparison group, changes or stability could reflect workload, case mix, staffing, or other concurrent conditions; these are possible explanations, not findings.

Needs revision: Reassess assistant triage against a simpler intake form and keeping the current process, including deferring or stopping expansion. There is no supplied evidence to rank the alternatives by effectiveness or cost. Any revised assistant trial would need a specific adoption barrier and a decision-changing test; another pilot is not automatically warranted.

## 3. Value and adoption
### Original assumptions and estimates — retained from 2026-09-01
Assumption: 80% weekly active adoption; 400 hours released per quarter. Claimed cash savings: $24,000 per quarter at $60/hour. No staffing or budget reduction agreed. Operating cost estimate $2,000 per quarter. Team lead owns operation. Staff must review suggestions.

### Reassessment — 2026-09-21
- Adoption: reported weekly adoption of 25% is 55 percentage points below the 80% assumption and 45 percentage points below the 70% expansion threshold. The reason for low adoption is unknown; easier work or staff demand cannot be inferred from this measure.
- Capacity: the reported projection of 100 hours per quarter is 25% of the original 400-hour forecast, a shortfall of 300 hours. This is the supplied projection; do not apply another adoption discount without knowing how it was calculated.
- Value: applying the original $60/hour rate to 100 hours gives $6,000 per quarter of gross capacity value. This is an illustrative valuation using the original rate, not demonstrated cash savings. No staffing or budget reduction has been agreed.
- Cost: the $2,000 quarterly operating cost remains an estimate, not an actual pilot cost. Subtracting it from the illustrative capacity value gives a conditional $4,000 balance, not net cash savings or an established return. Realized value depends on whether released time can be redeployed usefully; review burden and implementation costs are not quantified.
- Needs revision: Establish how capacity was measured, whether review effort is included, what useful work replaces the released time, actual operating costs, and adoption barriers before refreshing the funding case. Expansion affordability and delivery requirements remain unassessed.

## 6. Working draft and critique
### Author draft
We should roll this out to all teams. The pilot proves we will save $24,000 every quarter. People want this because it makes work easier.
### Critique — 2026-09-21
1. **Argument — “roll this out to all teams.”** The request contradicts the pre-set expansion gate: adoption was 25%, below 70%. The draft needs to confront that failed criterion and explain the decision now warranted. The supplied evidence does not establish feasibility across all teams.
2. **Evidence — “The pilot proves.”** A four-week pilot without a comparison group does not establish causation. Unchanged errors describe what was observed; they do not prove the assistant preserved quality. The draft also omits the missing evidence on shorter queues, the stated goal.
3. **Value and clarity — “save $24,000 every quarter.”** This uses the original 400-hour forecast despite the revised 100-hour projection. At the original rate, that projection corresponds to $6,000 in gross capacity value. Neither amount is demonstrated cash savings because no staffing or budget reduction is agreed. “Every quarter” also treats a projection as a recurring observed result and omits cost and redeployment uncertainty.
4. **Adoption — “People want this because it makes work easier.”** No preference or ease-of-work evidence was supplied. Low adoption challenges the expected behavior change, but does not establish why people did or did not use the assistant. The author's causal explanation needs evidence.
5. **Order and completeness.** State the decision in light of the results, then show forecast versus observation before discussing alternatives and the requested commitment. The present draft jumps from an expansion request to unsupported benefit and preference claims; it does not give the sponsor the evidence needed to assess the request.
6. **Repetition.** Repetition is not a material problem in this short draft. Resolve the argument and evidence problems before polishing wording.

Editorial choices remain with the author. No replacement prose has been drafted.

## 7. Outcome and learning
### Pilot review — 2026-09-21
Source: original forecasts and thresholds in section 4; user-supplied pilot report recorded in section 2.

The intended mechanism was assistant-supported triage leading to staff use, less intake work, released capacity and shorter queues. Actual adoption was 25% against an 80% forecast; projected release was 100 hours per quarter against 400. Errors did not change. Queue-time results, actual costs, and useful redeployment of released time were not supplied. The observed adoption and capacity results fall short of the forecast, but the overall effect on the original problem and net value remain unresolved.

Attribution is limited by the absence of a comparison group. The results do not show how much of the measured capacity release is attributable to the assistant or explain the adoption shortfall.

Decision quality and outcome quality are separate: setting the expansion threshold in advance provides a useful decision rule, even though the adoption outcome missed it. The lack of a comparison group limits what the evaluation can conclude. No later sponsor decision is available to assess.

Assistant recommendations, pending sponsor judgment:
- Continue using the original threshold and retain the pilot evidence for comparing options.
- Change the value assessment to distinguish projected capacity from cash savings; investigate adoption barriers and missing queue-time and cost evidence only where those answers could change the next decision.
- Stop treating expansion as justified by this pilot. Consider revising the approach, a simpler form, or retaining the current process before committing further resources. This does not record an operational shutdown decision.

Reusable lesson: Forecast capacity benefits through realistic adoption, and pair outcome measurements with a credible account of what would have happened without the intervention. Do not convert released hours into cash savings without a corresponding budget change.
